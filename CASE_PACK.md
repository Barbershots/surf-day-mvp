# Aircraft noise over Brockenhurst

## A residents' case pack for Bournemouth Airport

*Prepared by Brockenhurst residents using public flight data, 2023–2025. Not
affiliated with the airport. Sources and full method are listed at the end.*

> **In short:** Brockenhurst village sits directly under the landing path for
> Bournemouth Airport. Aircraft are arriving **lower than the airport's own rules
> intend**, and often **levelling off** over the village instead of gliding down
> gently — and levelling off is what makes the noise. It is happening to **more
> flights each year, and it is worst at night.** We are not anti‑airport. We would
> like to work with Bournemouth on sensible options: fly the quiet approach the
> rules already ask for, route arrivals to one side of the village over open
> forest, and limit night flights as the major UK airports do.

> **Why now — there is a live consultation.** Bournemouth Airport is currently
> redesigning its flight paths through the formal CAA process **(Airspace Change
> ACP‑2019‑43, "FASI‑South")**. The new **satellite‑guided (PBN/RNAV)** routes are
> hyper‑precise: instead of today's scattered flights, aircraft will follow the
> **exact same line**. If that line is drawn over Brockenhurst it becomes a
> **permanent, concentrated "noise highway."** This is the moment to get the
> design right — and the airport has legal duties that help us: its **Section 106**
> planning commitment to enforce continuous descent, and its duty under the
> **Levelling‑up and Regeneration Act 2023 (s.245)** to *further* the National
> Park's purposes (the "Sandford Principle" puts conservation first).

---

## First, three simple ideas (no aviation knowledge needed)

- **A "quiet approach" is a gentle glide.** The quietest way for a plane to land
  is to come down in one smooth glide with the engines almost idling. This keeps
  it **higher for longer** and **quiet**.
- **"Levelling off" is the loud bit.** If a plane stops gliding and flies level
  for a while, it has to **push the engines back up** to hold itself up. That
  surge of engine power is the noise people hear over the village.
- **Where our numbers come from.** Every aircraft constantly broadcasts its own
  GPS position and height by radio (anyone can receive it). We used **three years**
  of that public data. It is the **planes' own data**, so it does not depend on
  anything the airport tells us.

# PART 1 — The problem (and the data behind it)

## 1. Brockenhurst is directly under the flight path

When the wind is from the south‑west (which is most of the time), planes land
towards the west and approach **from over the New Forest — straight over
Brockenhurst.** The map below plots where arriving airliners actually flew over
three years. The bright streak is the flight path, and it runs **right through
the village.**

![Where aircraft actually fly over the New Forest](outputs/sweep/route_map.png)

> **Fact:** About **76% of airliner arrivals** come in over Brockenhurst. (The
> rest land the other way, from the coast side, and miss the village.)

## 2. The planes are flying lower than they should

The orange line on the next graph is where a plane *should* be if it were doing
the quiet glide‑down — roughly **3,000 ft** as it passes Brockenhurst. Each blue
dot is one aircraft. Most of them sit **below** the line: lower than a quiet
descent, which means louder.

![How high aircraft are, year by year](outputs/sweep/descent_profile_by_year_large_jets.png)

> **Fact:** The share of airliners flying **below** the quiet‑descent height over
> Brockenhurst rose **66% → 73% → 74%** from 2023 to 2025, and the **average
> height is falling** (about 2,900 ft down to 2,800 ft). There are also **more of
> them** each year (694 → 926 with a measured height over the village).

## 3. They level off over the village — and that is the noise

A true glide never flies level. But we can see many arrivals **stopping their
descent and flying level** right over Brockenhurst — which, as above, is exactly
when the engines have to work harder. Here is one real, recent example, built
entirely from the aircraft's own recorded positions:

![A real example: Jet2 flight over Brockenhurst at night](outputs/sweep/case_study.png)

> **Real flight:** A Jet2 airliner from Dalaman on the night of **12 October
> 2025** flew **level at about 2,050 ft directly over Brockenhurst at 23:28** —
> roughly 1,000 ft below a quiet descent — after also levelling off higher up. A
> stepped, engine‑on approach, not a gentle glide.

## 4. It is getting worse — and worst at night

Night is when it hurts most: windows open in summer, everyone asleep. Night‑time
airliners over Brockenhurst have **more than doubled** in three years, and more
of them are dropping below the airport's own minimum height.

![Night-time airliners flying low, year by year](outputs/sweep/night_year_on_year.png)

> **Night facts:** Night‑time airliners over Brockenhurst rose **263 → 332 → 583**
> (2023 → 2025). Those coming in **below the airport's own 2,000 ft minimum** rose
> **0 → 2 → 7**. Aircraft **levelling off** over the village rose **63 → 95 → 157**
> a year.

## 5. This is measured against the airport's *own* rules

We are not inventing a standard. Bournemouth's published rules (in the official
aviation manual, UK AIP EGHH AD 2.21) already say arrivals should:

- fly a *"continuous descent… **at all times**,"*
- stay *"**as high an altitude as practical**,"* and
- join the approach *"**without level flight**."*

They even have a **night rule**: between 2130 and 0630, aircraft should get onto
the final approach **no closer than 8 miles and no lower than 2,500 ft.** The data
shows this is often not happening over Brockenhurst.

# PART 2 — What we'd welcome working on with the airport

We want a constructive conversation. There are three options, and they are not
mutually exclusive — the first one costs nothing and could start now.

## Option A — Fly the quiet descent the rules already require

Simply **doing what the airport's own rulebook already asks for**: keep arrivals
**higher and gliding down continuously**, and **stop the low level‑offs**. This
needs **no change to any flight path** — it is about how the descent is flown, and
it uses the airport's own published policy. Higher and smoother = quieter over the
village, and it could begin straight away.

## Option B — Route arrivals to one side of the village (over open forest)

The **last few miles** of any landing have to line up with the runway, so those
can't move. **But Brockenhurst is about 10 miles out**, and that far from the
runway planes do **not** have to be on the dead‑straight line. They could be
guided to **join the final approach just north *or* south of the village — over
open, unpopulated forest — and only then turn onto the runway line.** Modern
satellite‑based navigation makes this kind of "join after the village" path
precise and repeatable. The green and gold dashed lines on the map in Part 1 show
the idea (illustration only).

We would **not** pick north or south ourselves — each should be checked for who
lives underneath. We'd ask the airport and its air‑traffic partner to **assess
both** and choose the quietest option.

## Option C — Limit night flights, as the big airports already do

Britain's major airports operate strict **night‑flight limits**. At **Heathrow,
Gatwick and Stansted**, the government sets a "night quota" for **23:30–06:00**:
a **cap on the number of night flights**, a **noise budget**, and an outright
**ban on the noisiest aircraft** at night. Bournemouth is a smaller airport and
isn't part of that government scheme — but it **could adopt similar measures
itself**, and many regional airports already limit night flying through their
planning agreements.

> **Proposed asks — we would welcome the airport:**
> **1.** Publishing how well it meets its own continuous‑descent rule for these
> arrivals, and committing to improve it (higher, smoother, no level‑offs).
> **2.** Studying a new **arrival route that joins the runway line to one side of
> Brockenhurst**, over open forest.
> **3.** **Limiting and enforcing night flights** — a cap on night movements,
> enforcing the existing 2,500 ft / 8‑mile night rule, and phasing out the
> noisiest aircraft at night, as the London airports do.
> **4.** Giving **Brockenhurst a seat** on the Airport Consultative Committee.

## Who can make these changes happen

- **Bournemouth Airport** and its **air‑traffic provider** — for how approaches
  are flown and designed, and for night‑flight policy.
- The **Civil Aviation Authority (CAA)** — runs the formal process for any change
  to a flight path (an "airspace change").
- The **Airport Consultative Committee** — the airport's community forum, where
  residents' representatives can raise this.
- The **New Forest National Park Authority**, our **parish and district councils**,
  and our **MP** — natural allies, since this is about protecting a **National
  Park** and its villages.

## How we know all this (in plain English)

- The numbers come from aircraft's **own broadcast GPS/height data** for
  **2023–2025**, via two well‑known public datasets (OPDI, run by a European
  air‑traffic body, and OpenSky, a research network). It is **independent of the
  airport**.
- **Almost every arrival is captured** (~97%). For the exact height *directly over
  the village* we rely on a smaller, representative sample, so our **counts are
  minimums** — the real numbers are **higher, not lower**.
- We are careful **not** to say every low flight is breaking a rule — some have
  air‑traffic reasons. **The case is the consistent, worsening pattern across
  thousands of flights**, not any single aircraft.
